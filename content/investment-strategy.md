<p>If investment terminology makes your eyes glaze over, here is the short version: we are professional worriers.</p>
<p>We imagine the personal and economic events that could interfere with the plan. Then we build a low-cost, diversified portfolio intended to do its job without exposing the client to a loss that would require an unacceptable change in real life.</p>

<h2>Start with broad, low-cost indexing</h2>
<p>Most of the portfolio begins with broad-market index funds. Decades of evidence have made us skeptical that an investor can reliably identify, in advance, the active managers who will outperform after fees. Indexing gives clients a share of market returns without paying someone to make promises that are difficult to keep.</p>

<h2>Use the research, but keep the claims modest</h2>
<p>Long-term research has found additional expected return associated with value and company size. Value companies trade at lower prices relative to their fundamentals; small companies have less room for error than large ones. We may tilt a portfolio toward those risks when the evidence and the client's circumstances support doing so. A tilt is not a promise that either category will outperform on a convenient schedule.</p>

<h2>Ask what happens if the investment plan goes badly</h2>
<p>Time horizon and tolerance for market swings matter, but they do not finish the risk discussion. We also ask what a bad sequence of returns would force the client to do.</p>
<p>Suppose one portfolio supports higher expected spending but creates a realistic chance of selling the house, returning to work, or making a permanent spending cut. A less aggressive portfolio may be the better choice. The extra expected return is not worth the risk when the bad outcome crosses a boundary the client cannot accept.</p>

<p>Current valuations also inform our assumptions. The price paid for an investment affects its expected return and risk, even though valuation is a poor tool for predicting what the market will do next month or next year. We use valuation to calibrate expectations, not as a theatrical signal to jump in and out of the market.</p>

<h2>What the portfolio is for</h2>
<p>An investment account is not a scorecard. It may need to replace a paycheck, fund care, provide a reserve after a business sale, or keep a surviving spouse secure. We cannot choose the investment strategy responsibly until we know the job.</p>
